    • A mechanical calculator using geared wheels to add and subtract numbers up to 8 digits.
    • Each wheel represented a digit; carrying over from one wheel to the next was handled
      mechanically.
    • Manufactured in small numbers, it was too complex and expensive to become widespread but
      was conceptually important.
  • Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz’s Stepped Reckoner (1670s–1690s):
    • Extended beyond Pascal’s design to support multiplication and division via repeated
      addition and subtraction.
    • Used the Leibniz wheel (stepped drum), a key mechanical element later adopted in many
      calculators.
    • Leibniz imagined a “universal calculus” of reasoning, anticipating later ideas about
      symbolic logic and mechanized thought.

  These machines showed that arithmetic could be automated with gears and mechanisms. However,
  they implemented specific operations, not general-purpose computation.


  2.2 19th-Century Mechanical Calculation and Office Automation

  As administrative and scientific needs grew, demand for more powerful and specialized
  calculators increased.
  • Thomas de Colmar’s Arithmometer (c. 1820s):
    • The first commercially successful mechanical calculator.
    • Used in offices and government departments for routine calculations.
    • Provided reliable, standardized computation power before electrical devices.
  • Table-making and scientific computation:
    • Large tables (e.g., logarithms, trigonometric values, navigation tables) were computed
      by human “computers”—people whose job was to perform calculations.
    • Errors and the high cost of labor motivated attempts to mechanize repetitive numeric
      work.
