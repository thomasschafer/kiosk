  The Sumerians, who flourished in southern Mesopotamia from roughly 4500 to 1900 BCE, developed one
   of the earliest known number systems. Their system was sexagesimal, meaning it was based on the
  number 60 rather than the number 10 used in most modern systems. This choice, whose origins remain
   somewhat mysterious, proved remarkably practical. The number 60 has an unusually large number of
  divisors — it is divisible by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, and 60 — making it
  exceptionally well-suited for calculations involving fractions. The legacy of this system persists
   today in our division of hours into 60 minutes and minutes into 60 seconds, as well as the
  division of circles into 360 degrees.

  The Babylonians, who succeeded the Sumerians as the dominant power in Mesopotamia, inherited and
  significantly expanded upon Sumerian mathematical knowledge. Babylonian mathematical tablets,
  thousands of which have been recovered by archaeologists, reveal a tradition of remarkable
  sophistication. Babylonian scribes developed efficient algorithms for multiplication, division,
  and the extraction of square and cube roots. They compiled extensive tables of reciprocals,
  squares, cubes, and exponential functions that served as computational aids.

  Perhaps most impressively, the Babylonians developed methods for solving what we would now call
  quadratic and even some cubic equations. The famous tablet YBC 7289, dating to approximately 1800
  BCE, contains a diagram of a square with its diagonals and an approximation of the square root of
  2 accurate to six decimal places — 1.414213, compared to the true value of approximately 1.414214.
   This demonstrates not only computational skill but also a conceptual understanding of irrational
  numbers, more than a thousand years before the Greeks famously grappled with the same concept.
