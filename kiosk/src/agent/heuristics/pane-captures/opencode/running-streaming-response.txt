  ┃  # History of computer science: complete overview and milestones
  ┃

     roots stretch back to ancient mathematics, logic, language, and even bureaucracy: all the
     places where humans learned to formalize thought, represent information, and execute
     procedures reliably. The history of computer science is therefore not just a story of
     machines. It is a story of abstraction—how people learned to turn ideas into symbols,
     symbols into rules, and rules into systems that could scale beyond any individual mind.

     In ancient Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and Greece, early mathematics and record-keeping
     created proto-computational practices. Positional number systems made arithmetic algorithmic.
      The abacus embodied state and operation. Euclid’s stepwise proofs modeled deterministic
     reasoning. Centuries later, Indian mathematicians developed formal arithmetic techniques and
     transmitted the concept of zero; Islamic scholars expanded algebra, algorithmic thinking,
     and scientific method. Even the word “algorithm” comes from the Latinized name of the
     Persian polymath al-Khwarizmi. Long before electronic circuits, computation existed as
     procedure.

     The seventeenth century accelerated this trajectory. John Napier introduced logarithms,
     transforming multiplication into addition through representation. Blaise Pascal and
     Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz built mechanical calculators; Leibniz also envisioned a “calculus
     ratiocinator,” a symbolic logic machine that could settle arguments by calculation. At the
     same time, scientific revolution thinkers pursued universality: compact laws generating
     complex outcomes. This ideal—simple formal systems with broad expressive power—became
     central to both theoretical computer science and programming language design.

     In the nineteenth century, Charles Babbage designed the Difference Engine and Analytical
     Engine, and Ada Lovelace recognized their broader significance.
